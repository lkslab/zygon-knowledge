Zonal market, redispatch and nodal pricing

Four buses: a windy, cheap north and a load-heavy south joined by two small lines. Three solves: (1) the nodal reference, (2) a copper-plate zonal market that clears as if the lines were infinite, (3) redispatch: the market dispatch is frozen and every unit gets an up-twin and a down-twin the operator can call at a cost, so the flows fit the real grid. The gap between (2)+(3) and (1) is the price of pretending.

Where power is made, where it is used, and the lines between
A made-up country. The windy, thinly populated north makes far more than it uses, the south's cities and industry use far more than they make. The market plans to move about 2 GW south, but only two 400 MW lines join the halves: they would run far over their limit.
What redispatch fixes: the market's plan against the grid
The market clears as if the north-south lines were unlimited and plans to send about 2 GW south all day. The green part the two lines carry; the orange band is what the operator turns down in the north and up in the south, hour by hour.
One zonal price against a price per bus
The zonal market gives everyone the same price. Nodal prices tell the truth about the grid: cheap in the north, which makes more than the lines can export, expensive in the south, which is short.
Daily system cost by market design
The zonal market looks cheapest because it ignores the bottleneck; once the operator pays for redispatch the day costs more than nodal pricing would have.
Redispatch volumes by unit
Northern wind is turned down and southern gas turned up so the north-south lines hold. Volumes are what the market schedule got wrong about the grid.
Line loading implied by the zonal schedule
Flows the market schedule would push through the real grid: the two north-south lines run far above their limit, which is the redispatch need.
